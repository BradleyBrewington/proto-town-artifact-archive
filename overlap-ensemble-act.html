Overlap-Ensemble ACT on the Pi

Continuous control from a 1-second brain: pipelined chunk inference with a freshness-weighted, fade-in ensemble.

policy act_v0 · 51.6M chunk K = 100 ticks (3.33 s) inference T ≈ 30 ticks (1.0 s, Pi 5 CPU) rate 30 Hz

01The problem this solves

The v0 policy maps one observation (two 640×360 camera frames + 6 joint angles) to a chunk of 100 future joint targets. Full-chunk sync — what rollout.py does — executes the whole chunk, then stops the arm for ~1 s to think. It works (roll002 placed the roll on the X), but the arm is blind for 3.3 s at a stretch and idle 23% of the time.

The overlap method removes both costs. Inference runs continuously in a background thread while the arm executes; every new chunk overlaps the previous ones, and where they overlap, their predictions are blended. The arm never stops, and its actions are re-anchored to a fresh observation every second instead of every 3.3.

02The pipeline

The planner thread captures an observation at tick tk and its chunk arrives T ≈ 30 ticks later, covering ticks tk … tk+99. A new inference starts the moment the previous one finishes, so chunks land every ~30 ticks:

Fig. 1 — dot: observation captured. Pale bar: inference in flight (~30 ticks). Solid bar: the chunk's usable window once it arrives. Only the first second is stop-and-go; after that the executor always has 2–3 live chunks covering the current tick.

At any tick t after bootstrap, the chunks whose windows contain t and that have already arrived number two or three — anchored roughly 1 s, 2 s, and 3 s in the past. That standing overlap is the raw material for the ensemble.

03The ensemble

Chunk k is a matrix of absolute joint targets âk[0…99] anchored at tick tk; its prediction for absolute tick t is âk(t) = âk[t − tk]. The executed action is the weighted mean over all live chunks, per joint:

a(t)= Σk wk(t) · âk(t) Σk wk(t)

with each weight the product of two factors — freshness and fade-in:

wk(t)= e−α·agek(t) × min(1, (t − arrk + 1) / F)
freshness (blue)  ·  fade-in (red)  ·  agek(t) = (t − tk)/30 s  ·  arrk = arrival tick  ·  F = 5 ticks

Freshness. A chunk's authority decays exponentially with the age of the observation it was computed from. With α = 1.0:

observation agee−α·agetypical role at that moment
1.0 s0.368newest chunk, just arrived
2.0 s0.135middle chunk
3.0 s0.050oldest chunk, window nearly spent

Normalized, a typical 3-chunk moment splits 67% / 24% / 9% newest-to-oldest: the freshest prediction leads, the older ones smooth it. α is the single reactivity knob — α → 0 is a plain average, α large is “trust only the newest chunk.”

This inverts the ACT paper's ensemble, which weights older predictions more (wi = e−m·i, i = 0 oldest) to buy smoothness. The paper can afford that because it infers every tick and averages ~90 chunks. Here, with a 1 s observation-to-execution latency and only 2–3 chunks, reactivity is the point — and smoothness is bought instead by the fade-in below. If it proves twitchy, flipping α's sign is a one-character experiment. Answered — see §07: the sign barely matters, the magnitude does, and the field optimum lands right where the paper's did.

04The fade-in math

The fade-in exists because of a discretization problem the paper never faces. Its ensemble is a sum over ~90 chunks; one chunk entering shifts the mean by ~1%. Ours is a sum over 2–3, so a chunk entering at full weight yanks the mean.

Quantify the yank. Just before chunk n arrives, the executed action is a(t) built from the old chunks. At arrival, the new mean is a convex blend of old and new:

a′(t)= (1 − λ) · a(t) + λ · ân(t) , λ= wn Σold wk + wn

so the command jumps by Δ = λ · (ân(t) − a(t)) in a single 33 ms tick. Plug in the α = 1 numbers: the arriving chunk has age 1 s (w = 0.368) against old chunks of age 2 s and 3 s (0.135 + 0.050), giving λ ≈ 0.67. If the new chunk disagrees with the running blend by δ = 5° — routine mid-grasp, where a fresh view genuinely revises the plan — that is a 3.3° command step in one tick: a visible kink, an audible servo snap, and a collision with the 4°/tick safety clamp.

The fade-in spreads that same correction over F ticks by ramping the new chunk's weight linearly from 1/F to 1:

faden(t)= min(1, (t − arrn + 1)/F) ⇒ λ(i)= (i+1)/F · wn Σold wk + (i+1)/F · wn , i = 0 … F−1

With F = 5, λ steps through 0.28, 0.44, 0.54, 0.61, 0.67 over 165 ms — the same authority handover, but the per-tick increment of the blend never exceeds ~1.4°·(δ/5°). The trajectory bends instead of kinking. The correction still completes in a sixth of a second, so reactivity is essentially untouched.

No fade-out is needed on the way down: a chunk leaves the ensemble only when its window ends at age 3.33 s, where its freshness weight (0.036) is already just ~7% of the total — its departure moves the mean by well under a degree.

Fig. 2 — one chunk's raw weight wk over its executed lifetime. Red: linear fade-in over F = 5 ticks. Blue: exponential freshness decay until the window expires at 3.33 s. Dashed: the step-function entry the fade-in replaces.

05Why it can't starve

The freshest chunk available at tick t was anchored no earlier than t − 2T (one inference just finished, one in flight), so its window reaches to at least

(t − 2T) + K − 1 = t + 39 ticks

— the executor always holds ≥ 1.3 s of runway, and the general condition is 2T < K: inference under 1.67 s. Measured: mean 1.00 s, worst 1.25 s. Even one anomalous 2.3 s pass doesn't stall the arm. (The v1 chunk-150 model, training now, relaxes the bound to 2T < 5 s.)

06Safety & diagnostics

Applied after ensembling, unchanged from rollout.py: every action clamped to the training-demo joint envelope (±2° margin), and no joint moves more than 4° in one tick. Same arm lock, same home-return on exit, same evidence trail (chunk-boundary JPEGs + full log.json).

Two new per-tick diagnostics, because they are the experiment's actual readout: ensemble disagreement — the max spread between live chunks' predictions for the same tick, i.e. how much the policy revises its plan when observations refresh — and late ticks, since the executor now shares 4 cores with a 2-thread inference loop (the bench says this fits; the dry run verifies it).

Finding 1 — the optimum is near-uniform. Task performance peaked at α = +0.5 and −0.5 equally; the sign of α barely matters, only its magnitude. Over a 3.3 s chunk lifetime, |α| = 0.5 is close to a plain average of all live chunks — which is exactly the regime the ACT paper's ensemble (m = 0.01, near-uniform) operates in. The §03 question is answered: the sweep independently rediscovered the paper's setting from the hardware side.

Finding 2 — large α is a start–recoil gait. At high α the arm begins a motion, then pulls back, once per inference cycle. Mechanism: each arriving chunk is conditioned on an observation ~1 s stale by the time it dominates; its early actions command a return toward where the arm was, not where it is. Averaging at low α absorbs that transient; near-pure switching replays it every second. Task success collapses in this regime.

Finding 3 — large α produces emergent mid-actuation recovery. The headline result. On a missed grasp or dropped roll, the ensemble — dominated within a chunk or two by post-failure observations — bends the trajectory back and the arm retries, without any reset. Observed latency ≈ halfway through the return-to-X move: one inference (~1 s) plus fade-in and weight accumulation. No other controller in this project can do this:

controllerblind windowon a missed grasp / drop
LLM loopentire actuationfull reset; cannot react mid-move
sync chunks3.3 snotices at the next chunk boundary, after the stop-and-think
overlap, large α~1–1.5 sturns around mid-trajectory and retries

The tension. One knob now provably controls two opposed properties: smoothness wants α small (Finding 1), reactivity wants α large (Finding 3), and no fixed value delivers both. That is the motivation for §08.

08Next — the disagreement gate

Separate the two jobs. Run smooth by default, and use the ensemble's own internal disagreement as a change-of-world detector: when the newest chunk — the only one that has seen the last second — sharply diverges from the older chunks' consensus, something happened that the plan didn't predict. That divergence spike is precisely the moment reactivity is worth buying at the price of smoothness. The base ensemble of §03 is untouched; the only change is that α becomes a function of time. Implemented in rollout_overlap_gated.py (wrapper run_policy_gated.sh, runs named grollNNN).

The divergence signal. With N live chunks, compare the newest chunk's prediction against the mean of the older ones, worst joint:

div(t)= maxj | ânew(j)(t) − Σk ≠ new âk(j)(t) N − 1 |

(div = 0 when only one chunk is live). This is deliberately not the max–min spread logged as “disagreement”: the spread can be inflated by one stale outlier, while this signal asks specifically whether the freshest information contradicts the standing plan. One tick of it is noisy, so it is smoothed by a first-order EMA:

div(t)= β · div(t−1) + (1 − β) · div(t)
β = 0.8  ·  time constant 1/(1−β) = 5 ticks ≈ 170 ms — one glitchy tick is attenuated to 20%

The gate: hysteresis + asymmetric slew. The gate state g(t) ∈ [0, 1] chases a Schmitt-trigger target with a dead band, so it cannot chatter around a single threshold:

gtgt(t)= { 1 if div > Don  ·  0 if div < Doff  ·  latch otherwise }
Don = 10°  ·  Doff = 5°  ·  placeholders — see calibration below
g(t)= min(1, g(t−1) + 1/A) if rising ,   max(0, g(t−1) − 1/R) if falling
attack A = 5 ticks (~170 ms)  ·  release R = 45 ticks (~1.5 s)

The asymmetry is the point. Attack is fast because reacting is urgent. Release is slower than one inference cycle (45 > 30 ticks) because the divergence spike collapses as soon as the pre-failure chunks expire — all live chunks then share the post-failure view — but the recovery maneuver has barely started at that moment. The slow release keeps the fresh plan in charge while it executes, then hands back smoothly.

Effective α.

αeff(t)= α0 + (α1 − α0) · g(t)
α0 = 0.5 (cruise — the §07 optimum)  ·  α1 = 3.0 (boost — the §07 recovery regime)

What that interpolation does to a typical 3-chunk moment (observation ages 1 s / 2 s / 3 s), normalized weights newest / middle / oldest:

An event trace reads: nominal motion at g = 0 → newest chunk sees the drop → div crosses Don → within 170 ms the ensemble is 95% newest-chunk → arm turns around → divergence subsides → over 1.5 s the weights relax back to the smooth blend.

Why the boost doesn't reintroduce the recoil. Two §04 protections survive intact: the fade-in factor still ramps every arriving chunk over F ticks (the yank analysis holds with λ scaled up), and the 4°/tick clamp bounds physical rate regardless of the blend. The gate removes the cause of the sustained start–recoil gait — the high-α regime is entered only for brief, justified windows instead of once per inference cycle.

Calibration criterion for Don/Doff. From a nominal α = 0.5 run with no failures, the printed div p95 must sit below Don, else the gate fires during ordinary motion. oroll002/003 logged 10–24° nominal disagreement at decision points, so Don = 10 may prove too low; override per run with --don/--doff. Every tick logs [t, Nlive, spread, div, g] to log.json for post-hoc plots of when the gate fired and against what.
